Robust Z-Score

October 2025 8 min read
Anomaly Detection Statistics ML

Z-score is a good old, statistically grounded technique for detecting things that are not "normal."

z = (x − mean) / standard deviation

It uses the mean and standard deviation of the data. In statistical terms, the z-score measures how many standard deviations a point is away from the mean.

Why the z-score value matters

  • Ideally, z should stay close to 0
  • When z ≈ 1, something looks slightly unusual
  • z ≈ 2 is rare, but still possible
  • When z ≥ 3, something needs serious attention

Events with a z-score above 3 occur less than 0.3% of the time under a normal distribution.

Simple example

Consider the following data:

[10, 11, 9, 10, 10, 11, 35, 9, 10, 10, 25, 10, 9, 11, 12, 10, 9, 11, 50]

Most values cluster tightly around 10. The z-score stays close to 0 most of the time — except at the spikes. When the value reaches 35, z moves to around 1. At 25, it becomes more noticeable. When the value reaches 50, z crosses 3. The largest value stands out clearly as something seriously unusual. So far, so good. Z-score works. At least in theory.

When things break

Now let's make things more extreme. Replace the last value: 50 → 100.

[10, 11, 9, 10, 10, 11, 35, 9, 10, 10, 25, 10, 9, 11, 12, 10, 9, 11, 100]

Intuitively, a value of 100 should look even more anomalous, and smaller spikes (35 and 25) should still stand out. And yes — when the value becomes 100, the z-score jumps to almost 4, clearly indicating a very rare event.

But here's the problem. Because z-score depends on the mean and standard deviation, introducing a single extreme value:

  • Increases the mean
  • Inflates the standard deviation

As a result, the spike at 35 now has a z-score of ~1, and the spike at 25 drops to ~0.5. They no longer look anomalous. One large outlier has redefined what "normal" means, causing smaller — but still important — spikes to be ignored. This is the core weakness of z-score: it is not resistant to outliers.

Making Z-Score Robust

To fix this, we need a method that does not let extreme values dominate the statistics. The idea is simple:

  • Replace the mean with the median
  • Replace standard deviation with MAD (Median Absolute Deviation)
zrobust = (x − median) / (1.4826 × MAD)

This is known as the modified z-score or robust z-score.

Why this works

1. Median instead of mean

The median is stable even when extreme outliers are present. In our examples, the mean shifts from ~14 to ~17 when the spike goes from 50 to 100. But the median stays at 10 in both cases. That alone prevents large spikes from shifting the baseline.

2. MAD instead of standard deviation

MAD is defined as:

MAD = median(|x − median|)

It measures typical deviation without being influenced by extreme values.

3. The 1.4826 constant

This scaling factor makes MAD comparable to standard deviation under a normal distribution, preserving interpretability.

The key difference in practice

With regular z-score: adding a larger outlier shrinks the importance of existing spikes.

With robust z-score: smaller anomalies remain stable, regardless of how extreme the largest outlier becomes. The smaller anomalies are no longer "washed out" by a massive outlier.

This stability is exactly what we want in many real-world anomaly detection systems.

When not to use robust z-score

Robust methods are powerful — but not always appropriate.

1. When data is truly normal

If your data is well-behaved and normally distributed, both methods produce almost identical results. The extra complexity isn't worth it.

2. When strict z-score interpretation matters

Classic interpretations like "z = 3 means this happens less than 0.3% of the time" do not hold cleanly for robust z-scores. Robust z-scores can reach very large values (30, 40, 60), which breaks probabilistic intuition.

3. When outliers should redefine normal

Sometimes outliers signal a genuine shift — traffic growth, seasonal demand changes, system upgrades. In such cases, you want the baseline to move. Robust z-score will adapt, but more slowly.

Final takeaway

  • Z-score is simple, intuitive, and statistically elegant.
  • Robust z-score trades some interpretability for stability under extreme outliers.

Neither is universally better. The right choice depends on what "normal" should mean in your system — and whether you want outliers to redefine it or merely be detected.
